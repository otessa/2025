Session 3.1

11:15 - 12:05 | Integrating Artificial Intelligence into a Community of Inquiry: The Roles of AI

Practice

Hyeyung Park | Athabascau University

Abstract

The Fifth Industrial Revolution (5IR) highlights the collaboration between humans and technology, emphasizing sustainability, human-centeredness, and environmental responsibility. Artificial intelligence (AI) is a transformative force reshaping instructional design by partnering with learners, addressing individual needs, and fostering personalized learning. This research explores AI applications in instructional design and strategies to overcome challenges in AI integration. Using a three-level model as a learning theory, it examines the roles of AI for individual, team and organizational learning. Furthermore, this study provides practical insights for integrating AI into a community of inquiry (CoI) framework to enhance learning design. The dearth of research on the roles of AI within the CoI framework has made it difficult for practitioners to employ AI in instructional strategies effectively. This paper addresses this gap by proposing AI roles grounded in a three-level model and offering practical insights on integrating AI into a CoI framework. Ultimately, it empowers practitioners to leverage AI effectively, maximize its potential benefits, and address challenges proactively through a theory-informed approach.

Session 4.1

12:45 - 1:35 | Designing Calm: Reducing Learner Anxiety with H5P in Digital OER Textbooks

Workshop or Demo

Theresa Huff | Loyola Marymount University

Abstract

“Anxiety is a pervasive challenge in online learning, significantly affecting learner engagement (Beattie, 2022), retention (Rosser-Majors et al., 2022), and success (Akpen et al., 2024). While previous research has focused on fostering belonging (Narla et al., 2024), enhancing peer and instructor connections (Hoferichter et al., 2022), and providing mental health support (Brewer, 2023), little attention has been given to the role of instructional design in addressing learner anxiety. This interactive workshop bridges that gap by demonstrating how thoughtfully applying four multimedia principles—segmenting, generative activities, feedback, and emotional design—through H5P and images in open digital textbooks can reduce anxiety and foster more inclusive, calming online learning environments.

This workshop builds on a qualitative study of 50 online college students who completed an interview survey exploring the application of these multimedia principles across six scenarios in a digital textbook. The findings highlight that all four principles helped relieve anxiety, with varying degrees of effectiveness. Segmenting had the most consistent impact, with H5P tools like the Course Presentation and Accordion being highly effective in reducing text overload, improving perceived efficiency, and giving learners control over content. Generative activities and feedback, as applied in H5P Quizzes, alleviated anxiety by promoting self-assessment and validating knowledge, though Interactive Videos sometimes caused anxiety due to interruptions in flow and a “test-like” feel. Emotional design, when applied using H5P Audio, helped reinforce key concepts and engage learners, but rounded, warm-colored images were less effective in reducing anxiety.

In this session, participants will explore examples of instructional materials that integrate these principles, comparing versions with and without their application. A hands-on segment will guide attendees in creating their own H5P content—such as reflective exercises, visual content, or formative assessments—and embedding it into a Pressbook. Participants can follow along or observe, depending on their preference. Open discussions will invite attendees to consider how these techniques can be applied to reframe “togetherness” in online learning, fostering connection by addressing shared challenges like anxiety.”

Session 4.2a

12:45 - 1:05 | Mid-American Conference Websites and Student Well-Being: A Content Analysis

Research

Hui Zhan | Ohio University

Abstract

In this study, we investigate the twelve Mid-American Conference (MAC) universities’ websites, aiming to assess how they articulate well-being components outlined in the inter-association definition of well-being. Our results suggest that universities are prioritizing different components of well-being from an individual and a community level, underscoring the significance of employing the inter-association definition to guide the assessment.

Session 4.2b

1:05 - 1:25 | Creating Whiteboard Animations to Explain Tooth Microanatomy

Practice

Nazlee Sharmin et al. | University of Alberta

Abstract

“The knowledge of tooth microanatomy is a cornerstone of dental education. However, teaching this complex concept in an engaging manner is challenging for many educators. The rapid development of digital instructional tools has enormous potential for the design and development of engaging and effective teaching resources; whiteboard animation is one of them. Whiteboard animation refers to a specific style of animated videos where the content appears to be hand-drawn on a school whiteboard and narrated in a storytelling manner. This technique uses cartoons and line drawings rather than live videos and realistic images. Whiteboard animations have all the benefits of traditional animations, illustrating an abstract idea, simplicity, and engagement. However, the feature that makes whiteboard animations stand out as an educational tool is their ability to combine visual thinking and storytelling (Adams, 2020).

Storytelling is a multimodal teaching approach that simultaneously engages listeners’ thinking, emotions, and imagination (Crocetti & Barr, 2020). However, this powerful tool is underutilized in higher education, particularly science education (Kokkotas, Rizaki, & Malamitsa, 2010; Krupa, 2014). Whiteboard animations, powered by storytelling, have been successfully applied to explain complex health-related topics for medical and pharmacology students (Larnard et al., 2020). Studies also showed that this animation technique positively affects the retention, engagement, and enjoyment of university-level students (Li et al., 2019).”

Session 4.2c

1:25 - 1:45 | Developing a Mobile App for Oral Histology

Practice

Nazlee Sharmin et al. | University of Alberta

Abstract

Mobile devices have become an integral part of our everyday life. The widespread use of this technology has emerged in the mobile-enabled teaching and learning era, also known as m-Learning [1]. m-Learning is gaining popularity in health professional education; however, reports on mobile apps targeting didactic teaching and learning of histology are scarce. Histology is an essential foundational component of dental and medical education. At the Mike Petryk School of Dentistry, University of Alberta, instructors use photomicrographs from textbooks to teach students the microanatomy of teeth, the development of tooth and facial regions, and developmental anomalies. The lack of high-quality tissue sections and insufficient class time challenge both students and instructors [2]. To provide students with an accessible collection of diverse histological sections and to facilitate in-class didactic teaching, we developed an Augmented Reality (AR)-based mobile app called Tooth ARcademy.

Session 5.1

2:00 - 2:50 | Speculation: Shaping the Future of Education

Practice

Danielle Dilkes | Western University

Abstract

“Higher education is currently in a period of great uncertainty, starting with the lasting impacts of the disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, and perpetuated by myriad new potential and actual disruptions emerging globally including Generative AI, climate change, global conflicts, changing public funding models, and increasing corporate influence on education, to name a small few.

In this time of great disruption, we are constantly, through action or inaction, in a global process of remaking the world and reshaping education. The possible futures of education are impacted by our collective social imaginations and by who has the power and resources to design the future (Benjamin, 2024).

Speculation, understood here as the art of imagining possibilities, is a powerful tool for shaping this future. Speculations can perform as a cautionary tale, similar to the fairy tales of the past, to draw attention to dark possibilities (Krutka, 2021) and focus our attention on things that we need to explicitly engage with or actions we need to take to avoid these undesirable futures or to “to give warning when we see ourselves drifting in dangerous directions” (Butler, 2000). It can also be used as a source of critical hope, to expand our minds to allow us to imagine radical change for the better (Houlden & Veletsianos, 2023). Importantly, speculation is a tool available to everyone, not just the elite, allowing a more collective approach to future design by those who choose to engage in speculative work.

This workshop will introduce the concept of speculative storytelling, drawing heavily on the work of Octavia Butler (2000) and Donna Haraway (2021) as well as speculative design methodologies (Dunne & Raby, 2013), future-making from design anthropology (Akama et al., 2018) and speculative education fiction from critical education studies (Houlden & Veletsianos, 2023; Krutka et al., 2021). We will look at how speculation has been used in various contexts as a tool for future-making and design and discuss specifically how it can be a useful tool for creating the futures that we want (and need) in higher education.

Participants will engage in reflection and micro-storytelling activities throughout the session, providing them with tools for speculative thought and empowering them to contribute to future-making in their own contexts.”

Session 5.2

2:00 - 2:50 | Transformative Digital Accessibility and Post-Secondary Teaching Praxis

Workshop or Demo

Kim Ashbourne et al. | University of Victoria

Abstract

“Digital accessibility is more than a technical/compliance issue. It is a dynamic sociocultural phenomenon that occurs when people with disabilities, and those using assistive technologies, can equitably and agentively: find, understand, navigate, engage in, and contribute to information sharing, information production, and sociocultural experience sharing in digital environments with others (Author, 2025; Web Accessibility Initiative, n.d.). It is dependent on the ways digital technology, content, environments, and experiences are designed, used, and shared but it’s also dependent on sociocultural factors. Digital accessibility is not often presented to educators this way, or as a topic for personal reflection, pedagogical exploration, or as a praxis of freedom.

If digital accessibility is presented to educators at all, it is typically presented as a set of technical how-tos relative to compliance with legislation, such as the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (2005). This technical/compliance-lens framing has done educators and learners a disservice by focusing on the “requirements” rather than on inclusion and transformative learning experiences.”
